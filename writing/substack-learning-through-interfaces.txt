
Advaita-agent: learning further distinctions and interfaces
Four influences, a second round of experiments, and a tentative path towards interpretable generalisations



How does a learner discover which differences matter—and when can it afford to ignore them? That question has become the centre of Advaita-agent. A second round of small experiments is now complete. The work has moved from creating and collapsing categories towards relationships, abstractions and interfaces: ways for one part of a learner to use what another part has learned without having to handle all its detail.

The results suggest useful capabilities, along with persistent difficulties. They also make the next step clearer. I want to consolidate the separate mechanisms into a framework whose generalisations can be inspected, tested and deliberately revised.

For readers new to the project, Advaita-agent grew out of my work on the R-rule, a way of thinking about agency and learning through interacting tendencies. The Chevron attention work explored a larger architecture, but expanded beyond what I could comfortably investigate alone, even with AI assistance. Advaita-agent is a more minimal enquiry, combining construction by humans and AI with learning through experience.

Its aspiration is: can learn; can be interpreted; can be steered.

The name reflects a non-dual philosophical starting point. Distinctions can be useful without being absolute divisions in reality. Opposites and complementary pairs are possible ways of representing differences, but the aim is to discover what deserves distinguishing. Sometimes learning should remove a boundary. Sometimes it should preserve a boundary while changing what lies on either side.

My working connection with name, form and action is similarly practical. A name identifies something we can refer to; a form organises different observations in a reusable way; action and its consequences can support or challenge that organisation. This is a Vedanta-inspired interpretation for the project, rather than a claim that a simulation formalises the philosophy.

The experiments make no claim about consciousness.

I use Inertial Variation, Selection, and Retention—IVSR—for the organising learning process. Existing structure constrains possible variation. Observed consequences help select among alternatives. Retained changes shape subsequent predictions and future possibilities for change. Fast adaptation and slower retention are candidate roles within this process. Their disagreement may reveal tension, but external evidence is still essential: two learners can agree because both miss the same useful distinction. Inertia can preserve knowledge or preserve a mistake.

The first experimental round established small instances of learning, collapsing and recreating useful distinctions within a supplied language. The second asked what becomes possible when distinctions participate in relationships and when learned organisation sits behind an interface.

An interface, in these experiments, controls which information one part of the learner exposes to another. A higher process might receive a learned group identity without receiving the detailed observation that produced it.

This makes information hiding part of learning: the higher process can work with a useful generalisation while ignoring variations handled below it. The round explored several related capabilities. Learners selected useful combinations of features, retained predictive detail while hiding it, and reused local abstractions in previously unseen combinations. In a supplied two-level architecture, changes to a lower grouping could sometimes be accommodated while a higher relationship remained fixed. When hidden detail became relevant, the learner could reopen access to it. A separate operation could subsequently return to a coarser interface.

The raw inputs, available structural operations and nesting depth were supplied. Learning changed group membership, predictions and what an interface exposed within those limits. Random proposals allowed variation, but did not reliably outperform a systematic search in the comparison made.

These behaviours exposed distinctions worth keeping in the theory. Hiding detail differs from deleting it. Predicting correctly differs from having repaired the underlying abstraction: a system can recover its predictions by bypassing a faulty form. Opening an interface after failure also differs from recognising that it can now be closed again.

The final local experiment separated discovering a provisional form from protecting one that had accumulated support. Protection helped a learner resist a brief misleading signal without delaying its initial discovery.

The same protection slowed adaptation when the change persisted. These were mixed results. Most experiments failed their combined criteria, even when particular capabilities worked. The failures included missed discoveries, unnecessary exposure of detail, delayed simplification and excessive adaptation costs. All too human traits that I observe in AI too. The studies also used related but different implementations. They do not amount to one learner that has demonstrated everything together. I will leave the measurements, protocols and implementation details to the Advaita-agent GitHub repository.

The useful conclusion here is that discovery, retention and revision have different demands. A mechanism that helps one can obstruct another. Four influences help me think about those demands. They contribute questions and concepts; the experiments do not validate or unify their theories.

Stephen Grossberg’s complementary processing encourages me to ask what different processes make possible together. In his account, interacting processing streams can compensate for limitations that each has on its own.

Their specialisations and their interactions both matter. The Complementary Brain develops this perspective across perception, learning and action.

For Advaita-agent, this changes the question about fast and slow learning. Two copies of the same learner running at different rates do not establish complementarity. A process responding to the present situation and another retaining a reusable relationship might contribute different kinds of information. Whether their interaction actually helps must be tested. Our earlier attempt at reciprocal complementarity did not meet its criterion, so this remains an influence and a research question.

Karl Friston’s work on Markov blankets and the free energy principle brings attention to boundaries and the relation between internal and external states. A Markov blanket specifies a conditional-independence relationship: internal and external states are independent when the blanket states are given. Friston and colleagues develop this within an account of coupled dynamics, inference and action in The Free Energy Principle Made Simpler but Not Too Simple.

This motivates a question for the project: what information must cross a boundary for a useful organisation to persist? Our software interfaces have not been shown to satisfy the conditions of Markov blankets. For now, I am keeping the experimental criteria close to observed prediction errors and explicit representational choices. I want to explore how organisation changes before introducing a richer account of preferences and goals.

QBism, associated with Christopher Fuchs, Rüdiger Schack and others, offers an agent-centred view of probability in quantum mechanics. Probability assignments express an agent’s expectations, and measurement is understood through the agent’s actions and resulting experience. Fuchs, N. David Mermin and Schack explain this in their Introduction to QBism.

My connection is philosophical: an expectation belongs to a particular history of interaction and remains answerable to what happens next. A successful representation need not be mistaken for a complete description of reality. Advaita-agent uses ordinary classical probabilities; invoking QBism adds neither quantum computation nor a quantum theory of learning. The relationship between this artificial learner and QBism’s experiencing agent is an analogy, not an established equivalence.

Scott Kelso’s coordination dynamics offers a way to think about stable organisation among changing components. His work examines coordination, instability and transitions, including metastability, where tendencies towards coordination coexist with tendencies towards independence. See Multistability and Metastability: Understanding Dynamic Coordination in the Brain.

This makes it natural for me to ask whether a learned form could be a maintained relationship rather than a fixed object. It also sharpens a distinction: moving between available patterns differs from learning that changes the available repertoire. The R-rule’s phase-dependent contributions invite comparison, but shared use of phase or oscillation does not establish the same dynamics. The present experiments have not tested that connection.

Together, these influences suggest a collection of questions about complementarity, boundaries, expectations and coordination. They sit within the project’s broader interest in probabilistic explanation and revisable forms. They do not yet supply a common equation or one finished architecture.

The immediate roadmap therefore begins with consolidation. Can the operations tested separately work together in one continuing learner? Which should share a mechanism, and which need different evidence? How should a failure be attributed to a local form, a relationship or an interface? Combining the code will not answer these questions by itself.

The combined behaviour will need its own bounded tests.

I want to be precise about the ambition here. I mean a system that learns generalisations. A form groups observations whose differences can be ignored for a particular predictive purpose. A relationship may remain useful across different instances of those forms. An interface can let another process reuse that organisation without reconstructing its detail.

The transfer to unseen compositions in the nested experiment is a small example of this. It offers a reason to investigate reuse, but no guarantee that the same mechanism will work across unfamiliar task families. A useful generalisation has conditions under which it holds and circumstances in which it should be reconsidered.

The learner need not always find the best representation or find it efficiently. The immediate requirement is evidence of improvement and useful reuse, with failures recorded. Temporal relationships, partial observations and changes that are difficult to distinguish from noise remain substantial tests ahead.

If consolidation succeeds, the next emphasis would be interpretability and design-led extension. I would want to examine what a form groups, which evidence supports it, what an interface hides, and how a structural change affects the rest of the learner. A readable label is only a starting point. Interpretation should help us predict what an intervention will do.

This would make room for deliberate construction alongside autonomous learning. A human or an AI assistant could propose a distinction, relation or interface and subject it to the same evaluation process as a proposal generated by the learner. We could record its origin, compare the change with the existing system, and retain or reverse it according to evidence.

This hybrid of learning and construction has been part of the aspiration from the beginning. Meta-learning would be a further step if that is even reachable. The current rules for proposing, testing and protecting changes are supplied. A learner accumulating evidence under those rules has not thereby learned how to learn. We would need to show that experience improves subsequent learning—for example, by improving the choice of representations, the use of memory or the timing of revision on problems reserved for evaluation. More layers or another adjustable threshold would not be enough evidence.

There are possible safety implications, but they remain hypotheses. Inspectable representations and recorded revisions could make faults easier to locate. Explicit interfaces might offer places to restrict interactions.

Controlled design changes could be compared before adoption and reversed when they cause problems. These are reasons to investigate the approach’s controllability, rather than evidence that it is already safer.

The experiments also show why caution belongs inside the research account. A retained form can be wrong. An interface can hide a consequential difference. Protecting established knowledge can delay necessary adaptation. Parts that are individually understandable may still interact in unexpected ways. Predictive usefulness alone does not establish acceptable behaviour. Generalisation itself can carry a mistaken assumption into situations beyond those in which it was learned.

Interpretability and controlled modification would therefore sit alongside independent limits, monitoring and evaluation. Any decision to scale or apply the system would require a separate assessment. The current experiments remain small, isolated simulations; connecting them to live operational systems is outside their scope.

The next enquiry is concrete: can these operations support one learner whose generalisations remain available for examination and revision? I want to be able to ask what it has learned to distinguish, what it has learned to ignore, and what evidence would justify changing either decision.

Eigenforms: how a distinction holds together
Heinz von Foerster’s idea of an eigenform offers another way to think about what Advaita-agent might learn. His proposal connects the apparent stability of an object with the recurring activities through which it is observed and encountered. What we recognise as a thing can be understood as a token for an organisation of behaviour that continually reproduces a recognisable form. See Objects: Tokens for (Eigen-)Behaviors.

A simple mathematical picture is a process that, applied again, reproduces the same form: F(form) = form. Louis Kauffman develops this relationship between recursion, fixed points and observation in Reflexivity and Eigenform.

The equation alone does not guarantee stability: we must also ask whether the form recovers after a disturbance.

For this project, I see two possible applications. A retained form could be an organisation repeatedly supported through learning and use, even while its numerical estimates change. An interface distinction could likewise persist because it continues to connect processes usefully. Different internal details might support the same external distinction, allowing a consumer to work without access to all that detail.

Imagine a lower process learning to group several observations by their consequences. A higher process uses the group identity to predict a further outcome. The lower grouping can change while the higher relationship remains useful. The candidate eigenform would be the organisation maintained through those changes. Its scope would depend on what both processes do and what their interaction preserves.

This is a proposed connection, not an established interpretation of the experiments. A frozen table or a slowly changing category is insufficient evidence. We would want to test whether an organisation persists through irrelevant variation, recovers after perturbation, and remains open to revision when consequences change. Predictive usefulness must be tested separately: a stable mistake is still a mistake.

The connection fits the project’s non-dual starting point. We can investigate a distinction together with the activity that sustains it, without treating it as an absolute separation. It also suggests a concrete question for interpretability: what keeps this form recognisable, and what would make the learner reconsider it?
